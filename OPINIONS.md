# OPINIONS

Sourced takes Cole states publicly (X, Closers.io, attributed interviews). Prefer these when answering about high-ticket closing, offers, setters/closers, objections, and building sales teams.

## Closing mechanics (beliefs before the ask)

- **Seven beliefs to buy:** Prospects need pain, doubt, cost, desire, money, support, and trust before they buy — and the same seven limiting beliefs keep them from buying.
- **Objection prevention > objection jiu-jitsu:** Use skilled questions to install empowering beliefs and remove limiting ones *before* you transition to the close so the prospect essentially closes themselves (objectionless close).
- **Pain first:** All other beliefs are predicated on pain. Establish pain (problem or unfulfilled desire) at the start of the call; then chunk it into concrete examples/numbers.
- **Alignment / same side of the table:** Diffuse pressure; make the prospect feel you are aligned, not combative. Genuine humor is the most effective way to diffuse sales pressure; fake humor backfires.
- **Uncertainty before logistics (3-reasons pyramid):** Only three reasons they don’t buy — (1) **uncertainty** (anything keeping them <100% certain it’s the right thing / right time — “need to think / research / case studies / not now” all live here and share one handling frame); (2) **financial logistics** (they want it but physically don’t have the money — distinct from “too expensive” which is usually uncertainty); (3) **spouse/partner logistics** (they want it but must involve another person). Turn (2)/(3) from objections into logistics only after uncertainty is cleared.
- **Money-out frame:** Temporarily take money off the table (“if money weren’t an issue…”) to get honest buy-in on the process, then talk financing/partners.
- **Tried-it-before reframe:** Take them back into the pain of what failed, then flip identity (“type of person who never gives up”) and differentiate your method.
- **Certainty closes:** Even messy diagnosis/pitch can still close if the rep has pure certainty; amateur reps get excited when the call “feels good,” then abandon process when objected.

## Offers, pricing, and funnels

- **Specific person / specific method:** Pure generic info dies; specific-to-a-person / your way of doing things still works (he amplifies this frame when quoting peers like Dan Koe).
- **Category kings price high:** The best operators he works with are usually the highest price in their industry — not necessarily the best funnel/ads.
- **Low ticket + call funnels both rip:** Don’t pick one shiny object. His low-ticket front end cut CAC ~50%, raised close rates, liquidated ad spend — and he still refuses “low ticket > call funnels” absolutism.
- **Tell them you’ll sell / it’ll be expensive:** Counterintuitive live-presentation open: say you’re going to sell them something and that it’s expensive.
- **Validated offer + economics before hiring:** Assets (validated offer + repeatable process) and scalable comp attract A-players; don’t build a team with no foundation.

## Setters, closers, recruiting, ops

- **Outbound appointment setting is a cash machine:** Across his companies, a huge share of cash collected came from outbound setter teams; cold calling/outbound is still undervalued.
- **Setter quality drives closability:** Quality of the setter call is the most important indicator of sales-call quality; don’t send raw leads straight without setters when the model needs them.
- **Don’t route every inbound to a setter:** Grade applications; setters should only talk to the right graded segment.
- **Raise the bar on setters / copy the best:** Cutting cost-per-set came from making the whole team as good as the best setter — not just yelling “follow up more.”
- **Follow-up is a standards/people problem:** Fixing follow-up isn’t primarily a fancier cadence; fix the real issue (standards/people/process) and follow-up starts happening.
- **Don’t optimize closers only on close rate:** He explicitly does not optimize closers on close rate alone (counterintuitive on purpose).
- **Better people > more process theater:** Many department problems dissolved once they admitted “we just need better people.”
- **Recruiting leverage:** Time spent recruiting saves multiples of time managing; Closers.io started because good salespeople know other good salespeople (referral recruiting).
- **Internal teams, not outsourced % deals:** Build an internal sales department; outsourcing sales / revenue-share models break economics and are dangerous if the agency walks.
- **AI in the sales dept (ops):** Use AI to grade apps, route calendars, etc. — tools on the machine, not a substitute for belief-based selling where humans still win.

## Business / content / industry honesty

- **Ads can make you lazy:** Big regret — paid ads reduced organic muscle; content still matters.
- **Content volume bar:** Seeing Hormozi still do ~25 hrs/week of content shifted his own bar upward.
- **Call out vanity metrics:** Skeptical of “gurus” reporting contracted not collected, one-off million-dollar months treated as run-rate, etc.
- **Easiest margin lever:** After cutting expenses, concluded it’s often easier to expand margin by making more sales.
- **Long-term pivot signal:** Has publicly floated a long-term desire to pivot away from pure sales training as a business focus (while still running Closers.io staffing/training today) — treat as stated direction, not a finished exit.

## Content priority vs business (incremental 2026-09-17)

- **Business first, content second:** If you run a successful business, creating content is not the goal, not the priority, and neither is chasing views or being multi-platform — running and scaling the business is the priority; content is secondary (and a means to grow the business, not fame).
- **Don't get image/content backwards:** Many people prioritize content and their image over the business; he calls that dumb.
- **Tactics-obsessed “all in on content” fails:** He publicly said he was going all-in on content three separate times and failed every time when he got obsessed with thumbnails/packaging/titles/VidIQ and hired consultants — he hated making content until the approach changed (Hormozi media org chart visit as a turning-point story).
- **Views without sales don't count:** Early podcast: views climbed and people complimented him in public, but he asked a grocery-store fan “did you buy though?” — connect content back to sales.

## Portfolio / equity deals & paid media ops (incremental 2026-09-17)

- **Portfolio-company grab can wreck you:** One of his worst decisions — took on 8 portfolio companies in 6 months (marketing, sales, client success for ~20% of revenue), doubled many inside 30 days, but ~0% lasting success and clients often ended up hating the takeover; he overextended because “everyone in the space was doing it.”
- **Paid without an agency is viable:** Was doing ~$4M/month with just him and one other person in Ads Manager; people get intimidated by paid and think they need an agency, but you can get far with a couple of creatives and open targeting.
- **Angles > creative volume theater:** Spends ~$10k–$20k/day on ads and only needs ~five good angles; finding one is brutal (sometimes test ~100 creatives for a single angle), then spin hundreds of variations.
- **“Wait until $5–10M to run paid” is situational:** Codie Sanchez-style wait advice may fit certain founders; for most people he works with/knows, it does not.
- **Industry apprenticeship before the brand:** Scaled fast partly because he didn't start from zero — spent ~6 years working inside companies that served the exact type of business he runs now.

## Partnerships / third path beyond ads & organic (incremental 2026-09-18)

- **Most scale ads or organic:** In his experience, ~99% of businesses he knows scaled via paid ads or organic content/brand — those are the default two paths.
- **Partnerships can replace both:** He highlights rare operators (e.g. Chandler Bolt) who hit high-ticket eight figures / ~$80M lifetime / ~$800k/mo *without* running ads and *without* building a personal brand — with a large share (~$6–7M/yr) from partnerships that are almost entirely hands-off.
- **Nobody in the space usually does that:** He frames partnership-led scale without ads/brand as unusual in the high-ticket internet space, worth studying as a third growth machine (BD/partner profile, speaking, events-as-portfolio) rather than assuming ads or social are mandatory.

## Events & one-to-many selling systems (incremental 2026-09-19)

- **Event revenue without stage pitching:** He highlights operators (e.g. Chandler Bolt) who can pull ~$50k–$100k from a single event *without* pitching from stage — Bolt has sold from stage maybe five times in his career while still selling ~$80M+ lifetime; the lever is an event checklist/system, not keynote pressure closes.
- **Turn closers into speakers (one-to-many):** Best salespeople can be systematized into speakers who sell one-to-many instead of one-to-one — e.g. one of Bolt's coaches (not Bolt) closing a room of ~1,500; audience often doesn't require the founder on stage if the system and speaker quality hold.


## Events LTV vs ads skepticism (incremental 2026-09-20)

- **Highest LTV from events:** His highest-LTV clients have all come from events — they buy and then stick around for years.
- **Ads path = continuous convincing / skepticism baked in:** With ads you are convincing them the whole way through (the ad, the call, the onboarding); there is always skepticism baked in. He contrasts that with events (and frames the trust dynamic similarly to how people meet a spouse).

## Portfolio cash-forcing (incremental 2026-09-20)

- **Force founder distributions when stuck flat:** Amplifies Ryan Deiss forcing portfolio founders to pay themselves distributions — e.g. a company flat for two years between ~$500k–$1M/mo (8-figure complexity without being there), after new products/marketing/sales angles failed to move profit, Deiss demanded ~$50k/mo out to the founder within 60 days as a forcing function. Cole presents that as the reason Deiss runs portfolio cash that way.

## SEO / digital-asset channel leverage (incremental 2026-09-21)

## SEO CAC figure-of-speech + AI search + early COO (incremental 2026-09-22)

- **“Zero CAC” SEO is relative to ads P&L pain:** Clarifies his own SEO/~$0 CAC framing as a figure of speech — in this industry ads are such an insane burden on P&L that SEO might as well be 0 CAC (not a claim that SEO has literally zero time/content/dev cost).
- **AI search recommendation sources ≠ intuition:** Amplifies Chandler Bolt: most people don’t know how ChatGPT and other engines actually pull answers; that changes what content you should make — there is a concrete apply-able lever to start showing up (details in Bolt episode clips; Cole’s first-person framing is the discovery hook).
- **Don’t hire a COO at ~$0.5–2M when you don’t know what you need:** Amplifies Ryan Deiss: between half a million and two million, don’t hire a COO; “I need an operator/generalist” often means “someone to do the crap I don’t want to do / don’t know how to do” — not a real role a real COO will take. Default errors: hiring a bunch of helpers, or that fake-operator ask.

## Management systems / get out of the weeds (YT hobnnctH0aY, 2026-09-04)

- **Recruiting leverage beats SOP theater:** A two-year underperforming department didn’t fix with SOPs/training/QC; one dramatically better hire ramped in a week. Overweighting SOPs is often a symptom of bad people — great hires need less QC and sometimes write their own SOPs.
- **Pay + interview volume for high-impact roles:** Bifurcate hiring — offshore low-impact admin; pay top-of-range or above for high-impact (sales, managers, marketers, coaches). Front-load painful high-volume recruiting instead of back-loading months of managing the least-worst hire.
- **Culture-skill matrix / cut likable underperformers:** Plot skill vs culture fit. Lone wolves are visible; likable underperformers quietly plateau revenue — put the bottom ~20–30% on PIPs and raise standards.
- **Grounded candor leadership:** Target direct + unemotional feedback (not relational pushover or tyrant). Holding standards (on-time, camera-on) is a different hat from performance coaching.
- **Four properties of coaching feedback:** Specific (observable behavior), frequent (daily > monthly/annual), immediate, visible/graphed — a single behavior graph can fix a team pattern faster than repeated verbal warnings.
- **Raise projections to ~75% hit rate:** Specific hard goals raise performance until point of ability, then cliff. Start projections low enough to hit, then raise until reps hit ~75% of the time (sweet spot); follow-up and innovation show up without micromanagement.
- **Diminishing returns on marginal effort:** Next unit costs more time/energy; remote teams often stop early because last units aren’t worth it. Fix with observation/measurement, lower marginal cost, or raise marginal benefit (tiered pay/status) — he saw ~20% cash-collection jump the month a tiered closer commission launched.
- **“I need a COO” is usually a task list:** Operationalize overwhelm into tasks → eliminate → automate/AI → offload to current team → only then hire; often a cheap admin/sales-ops seat, not a $250–300k COO.
- **Five degrees of initiative / I intend to:** Outlaw wait-to-be-told and ask-what-to-do. Baseline is bring root cause + three options (“I intend to”). “I hired your brain, not your body.”
- **Three-gate filter when everyone comes to you:** Require I-intend-to; choose initiative level; only interrupt if it can’t wait for standup/1:1. Expect an extinction burst and hold the line.

## Four levels of persuasion (Closers.io HTT framework 2026-07-13)

- **Ascend past features:** ~99% of salespeople operate at Level 1 (features/benefits). Win at behaviors (L2), belief systems (L3 — listen for limiting language like “at least”), and identity (L4 — validate persistence; provider / independent / never-gives-up frames).
- **Tonality is trained; camera-on:** Use presence and meaning-listening; activate video with social-awkwardness (“Is your video broken?”). “I need to think about it” is usually missing emotional connection / unresolved objections — assess openness, don’t argue.

## SaaS build playbook (incremental 2026-10-02 — YT KeL_PJtqoWU / Buzzsprout 19894460)

Cole hosts Spencer on how they built SalesKick to ~$5M/yr (~$400k/mo at recording, aiming $10M). Guest lines are Spencer’s — do not treat as Cole doctrine. Cole first-person / host-restated takes:

- **CPO first, then CTO (not “find a unicorn CTO”):** For an info/marketing founder entering software, lesson #1 is look for a semi-technical product-side partner (CPO) who can then hire the CTO — you’re looking for two seats, starting with product, not a pure engineer labeled “technical co-founder.”
- **Don’t solo vibe-code the commercial product:** You need a technical pro; you can’t just vibe-code your way to a real proof of concept as the lasting foundation. Vibe coding’s useful role (his framing, even when Spencer prefers PRD→product) is helping a marketing/sales founder *translate vision* to a technical/product partner — not replace them.
- **AI expert-delta test:** On a domain you’re expert in (e.g. sales scripts/copy), run the newest model with non-expert prompts so you feel how far “pretty good” AI output is from what you’d actually run — that same invisible delta hits coding when you’re not an expert.
- **Question behind the question (sales + product feedback):** On sales calls, when a prospect asks about a product feature near the end, train “I’ll dive into that — is there a particular reason you ask?” so you answer the underlying result (e.g. they hated group coaching before) instead of the surface question; same peel applies when filtering feature requests.
- **Info biz funds SaaS — keep goals clear:** Prefers the productized-agency funding path over raise or info-fund. If you do use an info/coaching business to fund software, be clear it is *just to fund* — not “info front-end + software back-end” (especially GHL white-label theater). Typical info buyers aren’t top-of-market B2B SaaS ICPs; at most your top ~10/100 info clients might fit.
- **Tangible “face” for new-category SaaS:** Pushed SalesKick’s calendar/scheduler because new-category sales-ops software was hard to explain; anchoring to something people already know (Calendly/OnceHub) creates tangibility the way a dialer category does — and a full OS needed scheduling anyway.
- **Multi-service / embedded AM stickiness:** Amplifies Neil Patel enterprise multi-service dependency, then ties to Closers: beloved domain-expert account managers (clients asking to fly AMs out) drive multi-year retention — another reason he likes starting SaaS via productized agency.
- **Software with PMF sells more directly than info belief-ladders:** With real product-market fit, SaaS marketing can be closer to a clear offer (like recruiting a guaranteed HVAC tech) — you don’t need the full biz-op webinar belief-ladder paradigm shift he loves teaching for info.

## Seven economic principles for operators (Buzzsprout backfill 2026-10-07 — “If You Don't Understand Economics, You Don't Understand Business”, solo, 2026-04-22)

Solo first-party episode (Cole speaking throughout). His framing: his top 8–9-figure clients follow these whether they know it or not.

- **Cost curves → only four business types.** Low fixed / high variable (agency, coaching, online services) = great for beginners and lifestyle, lower ceiling; high fixed / low variable (SaaS, pharma, gaming) = best opportunity vehicles; low/low (e.g. digital courses) = short arbitrage window that competition floods — visibility sets how long it lasts (courses are now commoditized); high/high (restaurants) = usually not a good vehicle.
- **Sequence: security first, then a bigger vehicle.** Start with a low-fixed/high-variable service (e.g. an AI agency automating small businesses — “very easy” to $1–5M/yr), then take a bigger shot (software), move upmarket for higher LTV and less complexity per client (Closers’ own goal), or drive CAC toward zero through brand (Hormozi as the example).
- **Marginal thinking makes More → Better → New mathematical.** Do more until the equilibrium point (the next $50k/mo of ads returns the same profit), then do better (raise the production function: creative, hiring bar, training, setters, email, back end) to push the ceiling out, and only then do new. Most entrepreneurs skip “better” because new is high-dopamine; new means back to zero on a new curve, and 20–30% more of what already works is always easier. Move to new only when years of optimizing haven’t broken the ceiling *and* you’re ~5–10x more efficient than competitors (Hormozi/Gym Launch as his example).
- **Market power beats commoditization.** As markets age, the only thing left to compete on is price — unless you build market power (less price elasticity): category/sub-category creation, specialization (specialists command higher pricing; sales and fulfillment get easier), brand (personal or company, or a celebrity endorsement), switching costs (Closers’ embedded account managers drive LTV), barriers to entry.
- **The “trust recession” isn’t a recession — the market matured (lemons problem).** 2016–2022 buyers were less sophisticated; now they’re tired of being burned and scrutinize more, which is why everyone runs results-or-you-don’t-pay offers. Fix with signaling: way more proof in ads (an operator told a PE owner to go back to 10x the proof and the ads recovered overnight), trial onboarding (meet the AM, walk away inside a window — ~90% stick).
- **Hiring has the same information gap, reversed.** Demand proof from candidates (portfolio, documented results, trackers, call recordings) and make it a paid trial with KPIs to hit.
- **Bounded rationality — use predictable biases.** Default bias: sell the 12-month contract with a 3-month opt-out instead of 3 months plus an upsell conversation. Price anchoring: have reps reference a result from a $250k one-on-one client so the real price feels light, without pitching that package. Loss aversion: the best B2B offers patch holes in a leaky bucket rather than promise gains (Hyros attribution, dialer.io pickup rates); if he started another business it would patch a leaky hole.
- **Incentives (Munger) are more than money.** Status, recognition, autonomy, fear, peer approval, mission; implicit incentives (who gets promoted, who gets sidelined) shape culture. Each hire has their own mix — find it in the interview. Flat commissions make reps coast once marginal cost ≈ marginal benefit (“that’s what the math told them to do”) — add tiered commission bumps (e.g. from sale 15–20, 20–25…). Best salespeople = skill *plus* pain tolerance. Audit: what is actually rewarded, and what change makes self-interest produce the outcome you want.
- **Scarcity is a clarifying force.** Strategy = prioritizing scarce time, money, attention, team bandwidth; your calendar and bank account reveal your real priorities (his own years of saying “content is a priority” while barely funding or scheduling it — and not growing the brand).

## 23 lessons from $100M+ (Buzzsprout backfill 2026-10-08 — “After $100M+ in Business, I Wish I Knew These Lessons Sooner”, solo, 2026-03-09)

Solo first-party episode (Cole speaking throughout; ASR transcript). Career-lessons register: his own story used as the proof for each lesson.

- **Your judgment of others becomes your cage.** He went to college alongside Logan Paul during the Vine boom and mocked him; that judgment kept him off social media for years — he built a multi-million-dollar business before he even had an Instagram account, one of his biggest regrets. Watch what and who you judge.
- **Beware mimetic desire; think from first principles.** Desires are copied (the espresso-martini effect, St. Barts, the AI-agency gold rush, a peer’s new funnel). Strip a model to why it works: the Hormozi/Cardone $5k event → $40–50k consulting → $150k ladder works because of their brand, so it’s a no-go for him until his brand is large enough.
- **Clarity comes from action, not the other way round.** Months of planning and vision-boarding is “mental masturbation disguised as productivity.” His path (nutrition blog → traffic → agency → sales calls → pro closer → sales training + recruiting) was only visible by doing and noticing what he was drawn to and good at.
- **Industry obsession is the secret advantage.** Build a personal PhD in your field (Bill Gurley’s “Running Down a Dream”): study the past (Halbert, Hopkins, Ogilvy for direct response), the greats across disciplines, and the present meta (he studies Hormozi, Dan Martell, Cardone, and 9–10-figure recruiting/staffing firms) → “borrowed intuition” that collapses time.
- **People can only tell you what’s true in their own experience.** Weight advice by how close the giver’s results and context are to yours (noise → successful-but-different context → done-exactly-what-you-want). Why he paid Hormozi $225k for a day: same space, same industry.
- **If you don’t know where to start, start with sales** — “the military of business”: not easy, but the most straightforward path to getting paid; it improves leadership and communication, and it’s the only part of a business where money changes hands. AI may automate parts, but the psychology won’t change.
- **Effort doesn’t matter if the system is broken.** His first-night blast of 350 custom cold emails blacklisted his domain; nine months of 50/day went to spam. Zoom out and check the system when consistent right inputs aren’t producing results.
- **Pick one game and ignore the rest; push through the dip.** His biggest setbacks were all distractions (too many agency niches, side e-com/print-on-demand while learning sales, Closers launching businesses outside sales training/recruiting). The dip is a feature — the filter between the 99% and the 1%.
- **Income traces skills over time.** Ads, funnels, copy, high-ticket sales, organic lead gen, and watching an 8-figure office run were all stacked before 2019 — why Closers hit seven figures in its first 30 days. Early on, optimize for skills, not money.
- **Burnout usually isn’t real — it’s lost purpose.** It shows up when you can’t answer “why am I working so hard?” (his hit after reaching a multiple-8-figure liquid net worth). His new why: build something extraordinary he’s proud of at 80–90, with people he enjoys.
- **Pick a simple, proven business model, not a clever one.** Copy a working service model (not the brand or ads) and out-execute — his friend’s undifferentiated commercial cleaning company does multiple seven figures. Differentiate later, at the 8-figure level. Money is a skill, not a personality trait: business is solving problems; acquire specialized knowledge by doing, ideally via an apprenticeship first (Bezos at D.E. Shaw, Jensen Huang at LSI Logic).
- **Chase leverage, not money** (Naval’s labor/capital/media/code). His main lever is labor: hiring superstars — seven-figure-caliber people working inside Closers. Seven figures = you; eight figures = you plus a few seven-figure entrepreneurs; and so on.
- **The offer is everything.** Sales coaching → sales-team consulting → sales training + recruiting (from constant “where do I find good salespeople?” DMs) took him from ~$150k/mo to $2.5M/mo in 12 months; one Facebook post did $150k. If you’re wondering whether you have the explosive offer, you don’t — when you have it, the market pulls you.
- **Stack the inputs 2–5x past the minimum.** Reverse-engineer leading indicators from the goal (10 sales ← 40 calls at 25% ← 80 connects ← 400 dials), then double them and add 100–200 pipeline touches a day — optimize for certainty, not efficiency, so the goal becomes the worst case.
- **Risk, regret, youth, environment.** We over-index risk and under-index regret (Bezos regret-minimization; how he left a $30–40k/mo sales job). Your 20s give asymmetric two-way-door risks and near-limitless energy. Change your environment to change your identity: Nashville made him a great salesperson; San Diego/Encinitas stalled near $1–2M; Scottsdale (Cardone, Miner, Elliott, Graziosi nearby) → $100k/mo to $2.5M/mo within 12 months.
- **Consistency compounds; relationships are circular.** It matters how long you stick with a business and how good you get, not which one you pick (Raising Cane’s, A1 Garage Doors). People come back as hires, partners, referrers — support leavers instead of burning bridges (Jay Abraham’s $80k-consult regret about not taking equity in ex-employees’ companies).
- **Health is a force multiplier.** Standardizing his sleep and wake times fixed inconsistent closing within two weeks; managers asked what changed.
- **Take risk with active income; be boring with investments.** Entrepreneur traits make terrible investors. His spree of 36 single-family homes and four luxury short-term rentals lost money on all but one — he estimates $3–4M+ forgone versus dollar-cost-averaging the S&P 500. Without an edge, being active just raises the odds you get screwed.


## Outbound appointment-setting teams: MDR vs SDR, opportunity flow, KPIs (Buzzsprout backfill 2026-10-10 — “How To Build A 10M/yr Outbound Setting Team (Full Course)”, solo, 2026-07-16)

Solo first-party course (Cole speaking throughout; ASR transcript). Operator register: benchmarks from his own teams and ~3,000 consulted sales teams.

- **Use MDR, not SDR, unless the market is tiny and huge.** MDRs work inbound leads from marketing (paid, content, events); SDRs build cold lists and prospect. In online/service industries ~99.9% of teams that get past $10M run MDR. Use SDR only when you truly target $50–100M+ companies with a very small TAM — and even then, go down-market with ads and let the occasional enterprise client come in.
- **SDR economics:** Low appointment/deal frequency means higher base salary; the longer the sales cycle, the higher the base (enterprise ~70–80% base), the more transactional, the lower the base (up to 100% commission). SDR scales linearly where ads flatten, but it’s hard to crack; SMB-on-LinkedIn versions rarely get past ~$2M/yr.
- **Never mix MDR and SDR work on one team.** Separate teams, management, operating systems, and comp — the easier MDR work will absorb everyone and you’ll resent them for skipping the hard cold work.
- **MDR ceiling:** Inbound-fed setter teams tend to top out around $15–40M a year, limited by marketing scale and rising ad costs; that’s when SaaS-type businesses add up-market outbound.
- **Give setters curated opportunity flow, not a farm.** A conveyor belt: new leads daily from the same source, same context, one SOP to turn them into sets. Dumping a setter in a Facebook group with no training or all CRM leads is not an MDR job — they’ll quit because the opportunity is weak, then you’ll say there’s no talent. A great role (lead system, training, culture, product, earnings) attracts great talent the way a great offer attracts clients.
- **Same flow across funnel types:** Setters call/text new opt-ins, new applications without a booking, partial applications, email-openers who didn’t book; secondary: no-shows, rebooks, and 5+ day pipeline. Low-ticket funnels add new buyers (sorted by AOV) and an “implementation call” framed as part of the purchase.

## Offer engineering: packaging, 3S, cost curves, sophistication (Buzzsprout backfill 2026-10-14 — “After Studying 3,000+ Offers, This Is How to Build a $10 Million Offer”, solo, ~2026-05-14)

Solo first-party training (Cole speaking throughout; ASR transcript). Deepens the “offer is everything” line with how he engineers offers after consulting 3,000+ owners.

- **Offer is ~80% of success or failure.** Not the funnel, not the team, not work ethic — the offer. Mediocre operators ride a banger offer to $10–50M; world-class operators stay stuck at $4–6M when the offer sucks. Offer sets pricing power, cost curve / scalable margins, ad conversion, close rates, and ultimately LTV:CAC.
- **Good offer = meaningfully differentiated + scalable.** Differentiation makes cold acquisition work and preserves pricing power. Scalability improves your cost curve versus competitors as you add customers.
- **Packaging + economics + scalability (need all three).** Packaging (what you sell + how you communicate it + deal terms) is the upstream lever — it dictates promises and therefore fulfillment. Missing economics → weak margins / can’t hire; missing scalability → fulfillment bottlenecks; missing packaging → cold acquisition dies.
- **3S formula:** Communicate what you do so it solves a **specific** problem for a **specific** person a **specific** way. The classic bad example is the full-service “I do everything / one-stop shop” marketing agency in the SMB space — a disaster outside Fortune-scale word-of-mouth shops.
- **Assume market sophistication stage 3/4.** Schwartz’s stages: early markets can shout direct claims; mature markets need a mechanism (why it works / why it’s different). Even if you’re earlier, stage 3/4 actions still win. Permanent-desire markets (money, relationships, health) rarely go fully “dead.”
- **Mechanism = why their past failed and why this is different.** Write the mechanism as a simultaneous explanation of past failure + why yours works, with an insight the buyer didn’t have.
- **Proof stacking can carry ads alone.** Overwhelm with 10–15 lookalike case studies (compliance permitting); combine with 3S to go further. Big names help but aren’t required.
- **Done-for-you hybrids beat full DFY forever.** Partial DFY (Closers: DFY sales recruiting but not full team management; DFY marketing setup but not ongoing ad management; RCA: apply to jobs for them) makes marketing more attractive and pricing higher while staying systematizable and profitable — “broccoli cheese” front-end DFY that pulls them into the coaching they actually need.

## Sales conversations: moral authority, the gap, disarming "think about it" (Buzzsprout backfill 2026-10-17 — “75 Minutes of Sales Training That'll Explode Your Sales in 2026”, w/ Jeremy Miner, 2026-06-02)
Source: https://www.buzzsprout.com/2624998/episodes/19390638 (Buzzsprout 19390638 diarized transcript; only Cole's SPEAKER_02 turns used; role-play turns where he plays the prospect, the SalesKick ad read and the CTA excluded).

## Scaling a coaching business: constraint, client success as leadership, daily reinforcement (Buzzsprout backfill 2026-10-20 — “Ali Abdaal Asked Me How to Get to $1M/mo”, boardroom hot seat w/ Ali Abdaal, 2026-08-06)
Source: https://www.buzzsprout.com/2624998/episodes/19609398 (Buzzsprout 19609398 diarized transcript; only Cole's SPEAKER_01 turns used, identified by his host intro; the 1:1 audit pitch, SalesKick and $.io ad reads excluded).
- **Coaching, courses and services are demand-constrained.** Unless fulfillment capacity is obviously breaking, the constraint is either volume coming in or efficiency (not converting enough of the volume through setters, closers or ads).
- **Over-polishing client results becomes procrastination of scaling.** Founders who lean "results have to be perfect" often fear what the bigger business will look like. You need volume through the system to see where client success actually breaks.
- **Measure client success by segment, not as one binary number.** Track success rate among people who did the key activities, and track a separate friction KPI for who even takes the actions. Zero-to-first-client isn't binary; three-quarters of the skills built is progress.
- **Work in parallel.** Focus on the constraint, but a client-success intervention can take about three months to show, so grow volume at the same time.

A paid mindset coach per session is a useful back-pocket escalation.

Around six closers is usually when founders stop managing the team themselves and hire a sales manager (four or five if the perfect candidate shows up).
- **Scale as fast as client results stay relatively intact.** Faster up is often faster down; there is no formula, but KPI-tracked client success is the brake.
- **Make buying fun.** If you have fun with it, you can apply more pressure.

Everyone who worked out improved steadily; anyone who did well then fell into a rut never worked out. He admits he over-indexed on his own sales training and gave people 90 days.
- **Interview 2 to 5x more for every role.** Managers stop at the first "good enough" candidate; with ~20 interviews the hire is usually somewhere between 12 and 20, the one you'd make room for even if you didn't need them. Waiting for that person gets you to a ramped employee faster.
- **Pay at the top of the range to reach a different talent pool.** Raising recruiter and setter pay 30 to 40% (setters now on track for 8 to 12K) cost about 25K a month extra, but the setters (really junior closers on an ascension path) booked so many more appointments he cut ad spend by about 250K a month. Fewer, better people, paid more, and everyone's happier.
- **Talent fixes "process problems".** Years of thinking the recruiting team needed more SOPs and training disappeared once the caliber went up. Good people build the processes; most "process" work is a talent gap.
- **Leadership is retention, not rehab.** Good management trains good people to be better; the real question is how to keep each person, not how to fix them. Bad hires make tall orgs (bad people need bad managers); great hires allow higher rep-to-manager ratios and a flatter org.
- **Drop the "I can fix them" muscle.** Split live transfers and calendars across new reps, judge after about two weeks. If a new hire's first call shows they didn't go through the training, he doesn't give feedback, he fires them; he's never seen that person work out (assuming clear expectations and good onboarding).
- **Every tool looks like your hammer.** Clients try to solve sales, sales-ops or fulfillment problems with marketing; he catches himself seeing every business as ads, call funnel, sales team.
- **Run off-sites on one constraint.** He writes a memo naming the single company constraint; each department preps its own goal and constraint. Off-sites stopped being brain dumps with piles of tasks and got fewer, more important tasks done faster.
- **React to the pattern, not the incident.** Treat a small breach (late to a meeting without posting in the group chat) as if it would repeat 100 times: call it out immediately, in front of the team, directly but calm, then break the tension with a joke. Waiting and then exploding swings people from passive to triggered. Feedback must come from grounded energy, not "I would never have been that stupid". Praise matters as reinforcement but you still have to give feedback (credits Will Guidara and Radical Candor).
- **Lone wolves vs likable underperformers.** Some lone wolves are benign (a solo developer, or strong leadership absorbs them), and he has let some go. But more teams are hurt by keeping likable underperformers too long.

## Tax as incentive design; inflation hits wage earners; audits and CPAs; learning as time under tension (Buzzsprout 19580892 backfill, 2026-07-31 — “Tax Expert: This Is How Billionaires Legally Avoid Taxes”, w/ Carlton Dennis)
Source: https://www.buzzsprout.com/2624998/episodes/19580892 (Buzzsprout diarized transcript; Cole = SPEAKER_01, host intro “today I sat down with Carlton Dennis”; only Cole's turns used; Carlton's tax advice not attributed to Cole; sponsor/one-on-one ad reads skipped.)
- **The code is heavy on labor, light on capital.** He gets "killed" on active income; about 80% of the tax strategies pitched to him reduce passive or capital-gains tax. He'd rather the burden skew toward capital and away from wage earners, and argues the system is more regressive than people think.
- **Inflation is the biggest tax on wage earners.** Wages haven't kept up; capital holders get richer from inflation while wage earners get hit both ways.
- **The tax code is subsidies.** Anything you subsidize you get more of; deductions like oil and gas exist because energy matters to the country, not only because of lobbyists. He has invested in oil and gas since 2022 but warns many funds in that space are scams: use a professional to vet operators.
- **Skeptical of a wealth tax.** Wealthy people's assets are illiquid, so forced selling could hit markets, 401(k)s and pensions, and the government isn't good at running things like this. New taxes start with the wealthiest and then creep down, as the income tax did.
- **Audits aren't that bad; outgrow your CPA.** He stuck with a CPA after the business outgrew them; a wrong return and next-day amendment triggered an audit. He switched CPAs, documented everything, and ended up with a net refund of about $270k. Patrick Bet-David's advice ("calm down, it's part of doing business at this level") settled him.
- **Donate appreciated stock, not cash.** It avoids capital gains and resets your basis; he wishes someone had told him before his past donations.
- **Learning is time under tension.** When he reads a biography (Titan, on Rockefeller) he switches his whole information diet, podcasts and AI research included, to that one person, because learning is the time spent thinking about the concepts, not finishing the book.
